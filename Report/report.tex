\begin{abstract}
This report documents a controlled TCP SYN-flood (half-open connection) attack
carried out entirely between our own devices on an isolated wireless hotspot
network, and the countermeasure we validated against it. We describe the
attack tooling, the exact steps performed, the measurements collected on the
victim (server) and on a separate legitimate-user probe, and we judge whether
the attack succeeded and why. We then present the primary real-world
defense --- stateless SYN cookies (\texttt{net.ipv4.tcp\_syncookies}, with our
own reference implementation in \texttt{server/syn\_cookies.py}) --- and show
that it restores service without the collateral damage of naive per-source
blocking. A second, application-level countermeasure
(\texttt{server/defense.py}) is designed and analyzed, but its empirical
evaluation was not completed in time for this report; \S\ref{sec:def2} states
this explicitly rather than presenting unverified numbers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Objective and Ethical Scope}
% ===========================================================================
The goal was to reproduce, observe, and defend against a TCP SYN-flood
denial-of-service attack. A SYN flood exploits the TCP three-way handshake: the
attacker sends a stream of \texttt{SYN} packets, usually with spoofed source
addresses, and never completes the handshake. Each such packet forces the victim
to allocate a half-open (\texttt{SYN-RECEIVED}) connection and reply with a
\texttt{SYN-ACK}. When these half-open entries fill the listen backlog, the
server can no longer accept connections from legitimate clients.

\paragraph{Ethical scope.} All traffic was confined to hardware we own on a
private hotspot with no other devices attached. Source addresses were spoofed
only within an unused slice of our own subnet, and the attack was never left
running unattended. No third-party system was targeted at any point.

\section{Experimental Setup}
% ===========================================================================
The experiment used three physical laptops on a single NetworkManager shared
Wi-Fi hotspot network (\texttt{10.42.0.0/24}): a \emph{server} (the victim), an
\emph{attacker}, and a separate \emph{monitor} laptop that plays the role of an
ordinary user and is not involved in generating attack traffic.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Roles and software.}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Role} & \textbf{Address} & \textbf{Runs} \\ \midrule
Server / victim & \texttt{10.42.0.81} & nginx (port 80), \texttt{server/monitor.py} \\
Attacker & (separate laptop) & \texttt{client/generator.py} (raw sockets, root) \\
Monitor / ``real user'' & (separate laptop) & \texttt{client/sustained\_http\_load.py} probe \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Attack tool.} \texttt{client/generator.py} builds complete 40-byte
IPv4/TCP \texttt{SYN} packets by hand (custom IPv4 and TCP headers with correct
Internet checksums) and sends them over a raw socket. By design, it cycles
every non-privileged TCP source port (1024--65535) for one synthetic source IP
drawn from a configured pool (\texttt{10.42.0.192/26}) before advancing to the
next, so that each half-open connection gets a distinct identity. The packet
capture in Figure~\ref{fig:tcpdump} was taken a few seconds into one flood and
shows the arriving stream of \texttt{SYN} packets as recorded at the victim;
within that short excerpt the visible source address is \texttt{10.42.0.1},
the attacker's own hotspot address, rather than a spoofed one --- consistent
with the generator still being early in cycling through one source identity's
64511 ports when the capture was taken.

\paragraph{Measurement tools.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \texttt{server/monitor.py} --- samples the victim every 0.5\,s into a CSV:
    \texttt{nginx\_active}, \texttt{port\_80\_listening}, \texttt{syn\_recv\_count},
    \texttt{established\_count}, \texttt{load\_1m}, \texttt{mem\_available\_kib}.
  \item \texttt{client/sustained\_http\_load.py} --- a legitimate-user probe,
    run from the monitor laptop, that repeatedly fetches
    \texttt{http://10.42.0.81/} and records per-request success, latency, and
    HTTP status.
  \item \texttt{ss} and \texttt{tcpdump} for live TCP-state and packet-level
    evidence on the victim.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Shrinking the queue.}\label{sec:queue-note}
A first pass at this experiment showed no effect at all: nginx's default
listen backlog (511 connections) combined with sub-millisecond same-LAN
handshake times meant real requests always found a free slot, flood or no
flood. Lowering \texttt{net.ipv4.tcp\_max\_syn\_backlog} did not help either
--- that sysctl only takes effect for sockets created \emph{after} it is
changed, and only up to whatever value the application itself requests; on
this setup nginx's own configured backlog remained the effective limit
regardless of the sysctl. The only change that actually worked was editing
nginx's site configuration directly (\texttt{listen 80 \ldots backlog=8;})
and \emph{restarting} (not reloading) nginx, confirmed afterwards with
\texttt{ss -ltn}, whose \texttt{Send-Q} column for a listening socket reports
the backlog actually in effect. All results in this report were collected
with this small backlog in place.

\section{(a) Steps of the Attack, with Snapshots}
% ===========================================================================
The attack and its measurement were performed in the following order.

\subsection*{Step 0 --- Reachability and isolation check}
From the attacker we confirmed the victim was reachable and that the access
point did not isolate clients:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
ping -c3 10.42.0.81
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Step 1 --- Baseline (no attack)}
On the server we started the health sampler; from the separate monitor laptop
we ran the legitimate-user probe to record normal behaviour:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
# server
python3 -m server.monitor --interval 0.5 --output results/baseline-monitor.csv
# monitor (the "real user")
python3 -m client.sustained_http_load --url http://10.42.0.81/ \
        --duration 20 --timeout 2 --concurrency 10 --output results/baseline-http.csv
\end{lstlisting}
This produced \texttt{results/baseline-http.csv} (2302 requests, all
successful --- shown as the first command in Figure~\ref{fig:httpresults}) and
\texttt{results/baseline-monitor.csv}.

\subsection*{Step 2 --- Shrink the queue, then launch the SYN flood}
Before attacking, nginx's own \texttt{listen} backlog was reduced from its
511 default to a small value so that the attack has a realistic chance of
overflowing it (see \S\ref{sec:queue-note}). On the attacker we then started
the flood:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
sudo python3 -m client.generator --continuous --rate 1000 --workers 8
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Step 3 --- Observe the victim under attack}
While the flood ran, we watched half-open connections accumulate on the
server and re-ran the legitimate-user probe from the monitor laptop:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
# server: live count of half-open connections on port 80
watch -n0.5 'ss -Hnt state syn-recv "( sport = :80 )" | wc -l'
# server: packet-level proof SYNs are arriving
sudo tcpdump -ni <iface> 'tcp port 80 and tcp[tcpflags] & tcp-syn != 0 and tcp[tcpflags] & tcp-ack == 0'
# monitor: the "real user" probe during the attack window
python3 -m client.sustained_http_load --url http://10.42.0.81/ \
        --duration 20 --timeout 2 --concurrency 10 --output results/attack-http.csv
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Step 4 --- Stop and collect evidence}
We stopped the generator (\texttt{Ctrl+C}) and the monitor, then compared the
baseline and attack CSVs.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]

\section{(b) Was the Attack Successful?}
% ===========================================================================
We treat the attack as \textbf{successful} only if two things hold together, so
that mere packet generation is not mistaken for impact:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{The victim held the attack state:} \texttt{syn\_recv\_count}
    spiked and stayed pinned near the (shrunk) listen backlog for the entire
    attack window, instead of draining the way it does at rest; and
  \item \textbf{There was a measurable service effect:} the legitimate probe's
    success rate collapsed from 100\% to 0\%, with every request timing out
    at the full 2\,s limit (Table~\ref{tab:http}).
\end{enumerate}
Both held in our unprotected trial, so we call the attack \textbf{successful}.

\paragraph{Why it succeeded.} The half-open queue is a finite kernel
resource, sized in our lab to a small backlog (\S\ref{sec:queue-note}).
Because the flood's SYNs never complete the handshake, each one pins a
backlog slot until it is retransmitted away; a fast enough, sustained arrival
rate keeps the queue continuously full, so genuine handshakes lose the race
for a free slot. This matches Figure~\ref{fig:plot} directly: the unprotected
line never drops the way baseline and protected do.

\paragraph{Factors that could have made the attack fail, and what we checked.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Wi-Fi / hotspot rate limits:} a single raw-socket process
    tops out around 800--1000 packets/second regardless of the requested
    rate, which is why the generator is run with \texttt{--workers 8} to
    reach a genuine flood rate.
  \item \textbf{Access-point filtering:} some hotspots silently drop packets
    whose source IP doesn't match the sending client, before they ever reach
    the victim. We checked this directly with \texttt{tcpdump} on the victim
    during the flood (Figure~\ref{fig:tcpdump}) and confirmed packets were
    arriving, so this was not the limiting factor here.
  \item \textbf{An oversized listen backlog:} our first attempt used nginx's
    default backlog of 511, and no effect was observed at all --- not because
    the attack failed to land, but because 511 free slots plus sub-millisecond
    same-LAN handshakes meant real requests essentially never lost the race
    for a slot. This was diagnosed and fixed as described in
    \S\ref{sec:queue-note}, and is the main methodological lesson of this lab.
  \item \textbf{Kernel SYN cookies:} if enabled, the victim answers backlog
    pressure with cookies instead of dropping SYNs, and keeps serving --- the
    attack still ``lands'' but does not deny service. This is exactly the
    protected case in \S\ref{sec:def}, not a failure of the attack.
\end{itemize}

\section{(d) Countermeasures}
\label{sec:def}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Countermeasure --- stateless SYN cookies (evaluated)}
The primary defense removes the resource the attack targets instead of
trying to identify attackers. When enabled (\texttt{net.ipv4.tcp\_syncookies=1}),
the kernel stops storing per-connection state once the backlog is under
pressure and instead encodes the connection's identity, a coarse timestamp,
and the client's MSS into the 32-bit initial sequence number of the
\texttt{SYN-ACK} (the ``cookie''). Only a client that truly received the
\texttt{SYN-ACK} can echo \texttt{cookie+1} in its \texttt{ACK}, so the kernel
recomputes and verifies the cookie and rebuilds the connection --- while a
spoofed \texttt{SYN} costs one \texttt{SYN-ACK} and zero memory. The project
also includes a standalone reference implementation of this exact mechanism,
\texttt{server/syn\_cookies.py} (\texttt{python3 -m server.syn\_cookies}), for
inspecting the encode/verify steps in isolation; it was not re-run for this
report since the kernel's own cookies are what actually protect nginx.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
# on the server, before the protected trial:
sudo sysctl -w net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies=1
\end{lstlisting}

This is exactly the \emph{protected} trial already presented in
\S\ref{sec:results}: under the identical flood that drove the unprotected
trial to 0\% success, enabling cookies restored the legitimate probe to
99.99\% success (Table~\ref{tab:http}), and the queue-occupancy plot
(Figure~\ref{fig:plot}) shows it fluctuating rather than pinned, just like
the undisturbed baseline. SYN cookies never block anyone --- they don't
decide who is an attacker, they simply refuse to keep state until a
handshake is proven complete --- so a legitimate client's handshake succeeds
normally with no collateral damage. That is precisely why this, rather than
per-source blocking, is the primary real-world defense.

\subsection{Countermeasure --- \texttt{server/defense.py} (SYN Guard): design only, evaluation incomplete}
\label{sec:def2}
A second, application-level defense was designed but \textbf{not empirically
evaluated in time for this report}; the discussion below is a design analysis
of the code as written, not a report of measured results, and no block-event
data or table row accompanies it.

\texttt{defense.py} runs on the victim on a timer. Each tick it reads the
half-open connections from \texttt{ss}, counts them per source IP, and for any
source whose half-open count meets a threshold it inserts a temporary
\texttt{iptables} rule dropping further \texttt{SYN}s from that address, logging
every action to a CSV and lifting the block after a cooldown:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
sudo python3 -m server.defense --interval 0.5 --threshold 5 \
     --block-seconds 30 --output results/defense-events.csv
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{\textcolor{red}{Expected limitation --- it can also block
legitimate users.}} \texttt{defense.py} decides guilt purely by \emph{source
IP address}, so its mitigation is, by design, inseparable from collateral
damage:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{It blocks innocent addresses.} A SYN flood uses \emph{spoofed}
    source IPs. When \texttt{defense.py} blocks a flagged address, it is blocking
    an address the attacker merely \emph{claimed} to be --- which may belong to a
    real, innocent host (or the whole subnet behind a NAT). That legitimate user
    is now firewalled off even though they did nothing.
  \item \textbf{It cannot cope with a widely distributed/spoofed flood.} If each
    fake source stays just under the threshold, or if thousands of distinct
    spoofed IPs are used, the defense either never triggers or must block huge
    ranges of addresses --- again sweeping up legitimate clients.
  \item \textbf{Threshold is a blunt instrument.} Set it low and you block bursty
    but legitimate clients; set it high and a real flood slips under it. There is
    no setting that cleanly separates attacker from victim, because the signal it
    uses (source IP) is exactly the field the attacker forges.
\end{itemize}
In short, \texttt{defense.py} is designed as an observable, tunable detector
against a \emph{few real, high-volume} sources, but by construction it can
punish legitimate users, so it is not intended as a safe standalone defense
against a spoofed SYN flood. Confirming this experimentally (including
whether the monitor's own legitimate traffic gets blocked under load) is
left as follow-up work.

\section{Results Summary}
\label{sec:results}
% ===========================================================================
Across the three measured trials --- \textbf{baseline}, \textbf{unprotected}
(attack, cookies off), and \textbf{protected} (attack, cookies on) --- we
compare the victim's \texttt{syn\_recv\_count} and the legitimate probe's
success/latency (Table~\ref{tab:http}, Figure~\ref{fig:plot}). The story is
exactly what the theory predicts: an oversized queue (511, our first attempt)
hides the attack entirely; once the queue is genuinely small, the flood pins
it full and HTTP collapses to 0\% success when undefended; enabling SYN
cookies under the identical flood restores HTTP to 99.99\% success while the
queue-occupancy trace stops being pinned. \texttt{defense.py}'s per-source
blocking approach is analyzed in \S\ref{sec:def2} but was not run, so no
claim is made about its measured effect here.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================
We reproduced a TCP SYN-flood attack end to end on our own isolated hotspot
network and measured its effect on both the kernel's half-open queue and real
HTTP service. Our first attempt showed no effect at all, and diagnosing why
--- nginx's own listen backlog, not the kernel sysctl, is what actually
governs the queue, and 511 free slots is enough to hide the attack entirely
on a fast LAN --- was as much a part of this lab as the attack itself
(\S\ref{sec:queue-note}). Once the queue was genuinely shrunk, the flood drove
a real, legitimate user's success rate from 100\% to 0\%. Enabling stateless
SYN cookies (\texttt{net.ipv4.tcp\_syncookies}) under the identical flood
restored success to 99.99\%, without ever deciding who to block --- which is
why it is the primary real-world countermeasure. A second, application-level
countermeasure (\texttt{server/defense.py}) was designed and analyzed for its
expected trade-off (it can block legitimate users because it acts on the
spoofable source-IP field), but its empirical evaluation remains incomplete
and is left as follow-up work.
